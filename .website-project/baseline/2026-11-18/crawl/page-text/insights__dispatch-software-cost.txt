What actually drives the budget Quotes for custom dispatch software vary by an order of magnitude because vendors are pricing different things. This guide explains the seven drivers behind the number, what quotes usually leave out, and how to brief a vendor so the quotes you get back are comparable. NOVEMBER 2026 7 MIN READ KRIVA TECHNOLOGIES TRUCKING & LOGISTICS FLEET · MORNING SCAN ILLUSTRATIVE FLEET DASHBOARD · SCOPE, NOT SCREENS, DRIVES THE COST "How much does custom dispatch software cost?" is usually the first question an operations lead asks, and most of the answers online are not much use. Vendor pages quote ranges so wide they could cover anything from a two-screen internal tool to a full transportation management system. The range is not dishonest. It is what happens when the question has no scope attached. A full dispatch system does not have one price, because two carriers asking for a dispatch system rarely want the same thing. Starting prices for a defined website, app, dashboard, or accounting sync are on the pricing page. This guide shows where a dispatch budget actually goes, so you can compare vendor quotes on equal terms. Every quote, fixed-price or not, is built that way underneath. So the useful question to ask a vendor is not "what does it cost?" but "how many people, for how many weeks, doing what?" Discovery exists because the cost drivers below are only visible once someone has watched the desk work. Seven things that drive the budget 1. The size of the desk you are replacing A dispatch board that reads loads from your TMS and lets the desk assign and reassign is a contained piece of work. A system that also handles quoting, order entry, carrier settlement, invoicing, and customer tracking is a TMS. Most of the cost difference between quotes comes from which of these the vendor assumed you meant. Write down which jobs the new system must own on day one and which can stay where they are. 2. Integrations Integrations are where estimates go wrong most often. Each one needs authentication, data mapping, error handling, a test environment, and a plan for when the other side is down. Typical candidates for a carrier or broker: The existing TMS, if it stays as the system of record ELD and telematics providers, for location and remaining hours Load boards, for posting and searching capacity Accounting, usually QuickBooks Online or Xero, for invoices and settlements EDI or customer portals for tenders and status updates Two integrations with good APIs and sandboxes can cost less than one with poor documentation and no test account. Ask each vendor which integrations they have priced and what they assumed about the other system's API. 3. Roles and permissions A dispatcher, a supervisor, a billing clerk, a driver, and a customer all need different screens and different rights. Every role adds screens, test cases, and audit requirements. Five roles is not five times the work of one, but it is substantially more than two. 4. A driver or customer mobile app A driver app adds a second product: offline behaviour, photo and document capture for proof of delivery, push notifications, and app store releases. It is often worth it, but it is a separate line in the budget, not a feature of the web console. 5. Data migration Moving customers, lanes, rates, and history out of spreadsheets or a legacy system is rarely glamorous and rarely free. The cost depends on how clean the data is. A one-time import of a tidy customer list is small; untangling five years of free-text load notes is not. 6. Compliance records Hours of service, fuel tax mileage, chain-of-responsibility evidence, and audit trails all mean storing more data, more precisely, for longer. The rules differ by country; our US, Canada, and Australia pages cover what typically applies in each. Building these records in from the start costs far less than adding them after an audit. 7. How you roll it out Switching a live dispatch desk over in one weekend is cheap to plan and expensive when it goes wrong. Running the new system beside the old one, desk by desk or lane by lane, costs more in planning and parallel running, and much less in missed loads. We almost always recommend the phased route; it is how we deliver. The cheapest line in most dispatch budgets is the dispatch board itself. The expensive lines are integrations, roles, migration, and rollout, which is why two quotes for "a dispatch system" can differ so much. What quotes usually leave out Before comparing totals, check whether each quote includes: Hosting and infrastructure: monthly cloud costs, backups, and monitoring. Third-party fees: map and routing APIs, SMS, ELD data access, and accounting or EDI connectors that charge per call or per account. Maintenance after launch: security updates, dependency upgrades, and fixes when a partner API changes. Ask what is included and what happens in month four. Change requests: how scope changes are priced once the desk starts using the system and asks for things nobody thought of in discovery. Training and handover: who trains the desk, and whether you get the code, designs, and documentation. With us, you own all three at close. Phasing to control cost The most reliable way to control the budget is to ship in slices the desk can use, and to decide what comes next from what they actually use. A common order for a carrier or broker: Phase one: the board. Read loads from the current system, show them with time remaining, and let the desk assign and reassign. No write-back yet, so there is no risk to billing. Phase two: the actions. Bulk reassignment with reason codes, an exception queue, and supervisor overrides with an audit trail. Write-back to the system of record. Phase three: the edges. Driver app, accounting sync, customer tracking, or load board integration, in whatever order the desk's pain points suggest. Each phase has a written scope, a weekly demo on a live URL, and a sign-off before the next one starts. If phase one solves the problem, you can stop there. Our dispatch CRM guide covers the patterns that make phase one useful. How to get quotes you can compare Send every vendor the same brief. It does not need to be long, but it should answer these questions: Which roles will use the system, and roughly how many people in each? How many loads, orders, or vehicles move through it on a normal day and a peak day? Which existing systems must it connect to, and do those systems have documented APIs? What must work on day one, and what can wait for a later phase? Where does the data live today, and how much of it must be migrated? Which compliance records must it keep, and for which countries? Then ask each vendor to break their quote into team size, weeks, and phases, and to list what is excluded. If one quote is much lower than the rest, the exclusions list usually explains why. If you are still deciding whether to build at all, read build vs buy a TMS first. Key takeaways Custom software cost is team size multiplied by weeks. Ask vendors for both, not just a total. Integrations, roles, data migration, and rollout drive most of the budget. The dispatch board itself is rarely the expensive part. Check what each quote leaves out: hosting, third-party fees, maintenance, and change requests. Phase the work so the desk gets a usable board early, and decide later phases from real usage.
