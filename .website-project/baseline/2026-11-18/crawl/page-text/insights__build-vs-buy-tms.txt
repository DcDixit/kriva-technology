ON THIS PAGE 01 When buying a TMS is the right call 02 When building pays off 03 The third option: build beside what you have 04 Comparing the total cost honestly 05 A ten-minute scorecard When buying a TMS is the right call Commercial TMS products encode years of other carriers' lessons: rating, invoicing, document handling, compliance integrations, and reporting. If your workflow looks like most of their customers' workflows, that is a large amount of value for a monthly fee. Buy when: Your workflow is standard. If a vendor demo looks like your desk on a normal day, you are paying for a product that already fits. You have no one to own software. Custom systems need someone to decide priorities, accept releases, and pay for maintenance. Without that owner, they decay. You need compliance features quickly. Established vendors often already integrate with ELD providers, fuel cards, and accounting tools. The pain is adoption, not capability. If the current TMS can do the job but the team does not use it properly, a new system will not fix that. Training and configuration might. When building pays off Custom software pays off when the way you work is part of why customers choose you, and off-the-shelf tools force you to work like everyone else. The signals: The workflow is your edge. A specialised service, such as enclosed auto transport, time-critical freight, or a niche quoting model, that commercial products model badly or not at all. Our auto transport work starts here. You run several systems stitched together. A TMS, two spreadsheets, a chat group, and an accounting tool, with people copying data between them every day. You are building a product, not a tool. If you plan to sell the software to other operators, you need to own it. Integration is the job. The value is in connecting systems no vendor connects for you, with an audit trail of what moved and when. The risk of building is real: a custom system is a commitment to maintain software. Budget for that from the start, not after launch. Our dispatch software cost guide covers what to include. The third option: build beside what you have Most operators who ask us about building a TMS do not need a new TMS. Their system of record handles billing, customers, and compliance well enough. What fails is the shift: the dispatcher needs bulk reassignment, time remaining on every load, and an exception queue, and the vendor screen offers one load at a time. So the real dispatch desk moves into a spreadsheet. In that situation, the cheapest and least risky option is a focused layer that sits beside the TMS: It reads loads from the TMS and writes status back, so billing and compliance stay where they are. It gives the desk the screens the vendor does not: bulk actions, exception queues, role-specific views. It can be rolled out to one desk or one lane at a time, and switched off if it does not earn its place. This is what a dispatch CRM is. It is also how the ShiftRail project was delivered: beside the existing TMS, with no rip-and-replace. A useful test: if you deleted the spreadsheet your dispatchers keep beside the TMS, what would break? Whatever breaks is the scope of the layer you need, and it is usually much smaller than a TMS. Comparing the total cost honestly Subscription prices and build quotes are not directly comparable. If three dispatchers each spend an hour a day maintaining a spreadsheet beside the TMS, that is a real recurring cost, and it belongs in the "buy" column. A ten-minute scorecard Answer yes or no to each question. Does a vendor demo look like your desk on a normal day? Is your workflow the same as most operators of your size? Is there no one in the business who could own a software roadmap? Is the main problem that people do not use the current system properly? Does your team maintain a spreadsheet or chat thread beside the TMS every day? Do customers choose you partly because of how you work? Do you need to connect systems that no vendor connects for you? Do you plan to offer the software to other operators? Mostly yes to questions 1 to 4: buy, and invest in configuration and training. Mostly yes to 5 to 8: a custom layer, or a full build, is worth scoping. A mix, which is the most common result, usually points to building beside the system you have. Key takeaways Buy when your workflow is standard and no one can own a software roadmap. Build when the workflow is your competitive edge, or the job is connecting systems no vendor connects. Most operators need neither a new TMS nor a full build, but a focused dispatch layer beside the TMS they have. Compare over three years and include the cost of workarounds, not just licences and build quotes. Not sure which side you are on?
