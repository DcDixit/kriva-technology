For logistics software, neither tells you much. A dispatch tool fails when it meets a real shift: a weak signal in the yard, a load reassigned at 6 a.m., or an invoice that can't go out because the proof of delivery never arrived. What you need to find out is whether a vendor can handle that, and whether you will know early if they can't. First, decide whether you need a vendor at all If your freight and workflows are fairly standard, a boxed TMS will usually be cheaper and faster. A good partner should be willing to say that. Custom software pays off when your workflow is your edge, or when spreadsheets are making your core decisions. Our build vs buy guide walks through that decision. What to check Do they understand your work? Ask them to describe what happens when a load goes wrong. They should talk about exceptions, ownership, and timing, not just screens. Will they look at the desk? Software built only from a requirements document tends to miss the workarounds. Ask how they will observe the people who do the job. Can they work beside your current system? Replacing everything at once is the riskiest option. Ask how they would run new screens next to your TMS during rollout. Have they handled integrations? Ask what happens when a QuickBooks or Xero sync fails. The answer should cover retries, visibility, and reconciliation. Our integration guide lists what a production-ready sync needs. Who owns the result? Get code, design file, and documentation ownership in writing before work starts. Questions to ask on the first call Who exactly works on my project each week, and can I meet them now? Do you subcontract any of the work? When will I first see working software, and how often after that? How do you handle scope changes mid-project? How do you use AI tools, and what does a person always review? Which of your case studies have client-approved numbers, and which are representative? What happens after launch if you are no longer involved? Red flags A fixed quote before anyone has asked how your operation works. Only salespeople on the call, with no access to the people doing the work. Impressive numbers with no way to check them, and no client willing to talk. Nobody ever suggests you might not need to build something. A big reveal at the end instead of working software along the way. Vague answers about who owns the code. Compare the quotes properly Quotes can differ widely because they cover different things. Before comparing them, check that each one states what is included for integrations, QA, data migration, training, and support after launch. Our guide to dispatch software cost lists what often gets left out. Start small You don't need to commit to the whole build to learn whether a vendor is any good. A short discovery phase should give you a written scope and a realistic timeline that you can keep and use with anyone, even if you don't continue. At KRIVA that phase takes one to two weeks, and our process shows what you sign off at each stage.
