Who product design is for, what we deliver, and where other services fit better

Who it's for

Founders and product leads building a dispatch or TMS-style platform, a fleet or driver product, or a B2B SaaS product where several roles share one system. Teams with a pitch deck and a few hero screens, but no agreed picture of the rest.

What we deliver

A role and journey map, one clickable prototype, and launch-ready UI with specs, including exceptions, permissions, settings, and the back-office screens that usually arrive late.

When another service fits better

One module or workflow in a live product: UI/UX design. Evidence before deciding: UX research. Tenants, roles, and billing on a live SaaS: SaaS product design.

KRIVA is a product design and engineering studio. You own the designs, prototypes, and documentation after the engagement.

Where logistics products break

The seams between roles,
not the hero screens.

Most freight and SaaS products look fine on the main screen. The trouble sits where one role hands work to another.

01

Dispatcher, driver, and customer designed in separate files

"Delivered" means one thing on the console, another in the driver app, and a third on the customer's page. The mismatch shows up as phone calls.IMPACT: status disputes, manual follow-up

02

Exceptions designed last

Late pickups, missing proof of delivery, rejected loads, and rate disputes are the daily work, yet they are rarely in the first prototype.IMPACT: engineering improvises the hardest screens in code

03

The back office is an afterthought

Invoicing, settlement, permissions, and admin tools get one sprint at the end, so the people who run the business work around the product.IMPACT: spreadsheets creep back in

Engineering-ready handoff

Every surface ships in one Figma file with states, edge cases, and annotated specs, so settings and exceptions are not sprint-two tickets. Design systems work is added when the product needs a kit engineers can build against.

How we run it

Map. Walk. Ship.

Three phases. Wireframing and prototyping sits inside the walk phase when you need it on its own.

  1. 01 · Map

    Roles, tasks, and exceptions

    A task inventory per role, the handoffs between roles, and a ranked list of the exceptions that cost the most time.

  2. 02 · Walk

    One shared prototype

    A clickable path with failures and permissions included, walked through with your team once instead of argued every sprint.

  3. 03 · Ship

    Launch-ready UI and specs

    Final screens, components, and annotated handoff that engineering can build against.

You provide: access to the people who do the work (a dispatcher, a driver or carrier contact, someone from billing), any current screens or spreadsheets, and one decision-maker. Limits: findings from a few conversations give direction, not proof, and we flag what needs real user testing.
