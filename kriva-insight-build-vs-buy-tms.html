Build vs buy a TMS: when custom dispatch software pays off

Most carriers and brokers should buy their TMS. Some should build. Many should do neither, and build a focused layer beside the system they already have. This guide sets out how to tell which one you are.

November 2026 6 min read Dixit Panchal · Founder & Design Lead Trucking & Logistics

The build-versus-buy question usually arrives after a bad month: a missed load, a billing error, a dispatcher who quit because the tools made the job harder than it needed to be. At that point both options look attractive. A new TMS promises everything in one place; a custom system promises software that finally fits.

We build custom dispatch software, so it is fair to say where we stand. For a lot of operators the honest answer is to buy, and we will say so on a first call. This guide is the framework we use to decide.

When buying a TMS is the right call

Commercial TMS products encode years of other carriers' lessons: rating, invoicing, document handling, compliance integrations, and reporting. If your workflow looks like most of their customers' workflows, that is a large amount of value for a monthly fee. Buy when:

  • Your workflow is standard. If a vendor demo looks like your desk on a normal day, you are paying for a product that already fits.
  • You have no one to own software. Custom systems need someone to decide priorities, accept releases, and pay for maintenance. Without that owner, they decay.
  • You need compliance features quickly. Established vendors often already integrate with ELD providers, fuel cards, and accounting tools.
  • The pain is adoption, not capability. If the current TMS can do the job but the team does not use it properly, a new system will not fix that. Training and configuration might.

When building pays off

Custom software pays off when the way you work is part of why customers choose you, and off-the-shelf tools force you to work like everyone else. The signals:

  • The workflow is your edge. A specialised service, such as enclosed auto transport, time-critical freight, or a niche quoting model, that commercial products model badly or not at all. Our auto transport work starts here.
  • You run several systems stitched together. A TMS, two spreadsheets, a chat group, and an accounting tool, with people copying data between them every day.
  • You are building a product, not a tool. If you plan to sell the software to other operators, you need to own it.
  • Integration is the job. The value is in connecting systems no vendor connects for you, with an audit trail of what moved and when.

The risk of building is real: a custom system is a commitment to maintain software. Budget for that from the start, not after launch. Our dispatch software cost guide covers what to include.

The third option: build beside what you have

Most operators who ask us about building a TMS do not need a new TMS. Their system of record handles billing, customers, and compliance well enough. What fails is the shift: the dispatcher needs bulk reassignment, time remaining on every load, and an exception queue, and the vendor screen offers one load at a time. So the real dispatch desk moves into a spreadsheet.

In that situation, the cheapest and least risky option is a focused layer that sits beside the TMS:

  • It reads loads from the TMS and writes status back, so billing and compliance stay where they are.
  • It gives the desk the screens the vendor does not: bulk actions, exception queues, role-specific views.
  • It can be rolled out to one desk or one lane at a time, and switched off if it does not earn its place.

This is what a dispatch CRM is. It is also how the ShiftRail project was delivered: beside the existing TMS, with no rip-and-replace.

A useful test: if you deleted the spreadsheet your dispatchers keep beside the TMS, what would break? Whatever breaks is the scope of the layer you need, and it is usually much smaller than a TMS.

Comparing the total cost honestly

Subscription prices and build quotes are not directly comparable. Line them up over three years and include:

What to include in a three-year comparison
CostBuyBuild
UpfrontSetup, configuration, data migration, trainingDiscovery, design, build, data migration, training
RecurringPer-user or per-truck subscription, add-on modules, price risesHosting, third-party API fees, maintenance and support
ChangeWaiting for the vendor's roadmap, or paying for customisationPaying for each change, on your own schedule
WorkaroundsStaff time spent on spreadsheets beside the systemUsually lower, if the build was scoped around the real workflow
ExitData export limits, contract terms, retrainingYou own the code and data; the risk is losing the people who know it

The line people most often leave out is workarounds. If three dispatchers each spend an hour a day maintaining a spreadsheet beside the TMS, that is a real recurring cost, and it belongs in the "buy" column.

A ten-minute scorecard

Answer yes or no to each question.

  1. Does a vendor demo look like your desk on a normal day?
  2. Is your workflow the same as most operators of your size?
  3. Is there no one in the business who could own a software roadmap?
  4. Is the main problem that people do not use the current system properly?
  5. Does your team maintain a spreadsheet or chat thread beside the TMS every day?
  6. Do customers choose you partly because of how you work?
  7. Do you need to connect systems that no vendor connects for you?
  8. Do you plan to offer the software to other operators?

Mostly yes to questions 1 to 4: buy, and invest in configuration and training. Mostly yes to 5 to 8: a custom layer, or a full build, is worth scoping. A mix, which is the most common result, usually points to building beside the system you have.

Key takeaways

  • Buy when your workflow is standard and no one can own a software roadmap.
  • Build when the workflow is your competitive edge, or the job is connecting systems no vendor connects.
  • Most operators need neither a new TMS nor a full build, but a focused dispatch layer beside the TMS they have.
  • Compare over three years and include the cost of workarounds, not just licences and build quotes.
